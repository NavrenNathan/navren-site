Don’t Be Camera Shy: Why Founders Need to Step Into the Spotlight

N By Navren · 13 November 2026

We write for the TL;DR crowd:

This article was intentionally written to be easy to skim.

If you only read the headings, bolded text, key takeaways, and examples, you’ll still understand the main ideas.

Read all of it. Read half of it. Skim the good parts. Whatever works.

You built a business. One you’re quite proud of. You make important decisions, manage employees and budgets, solve problems, and talk to customers every single day.

But ask you to record a 30-second video introducing yourself? Cue the cold sweats.

Chances are, you’d rather do anything else.

If that sounds like you, you’re not alone. Plenty of successful founders, CEOs, and business owners are perfectly comfortable leading an organization but deeply uncomfortable being its public face.

They’d rather let their work speak for itself. And to a certain extent, it should. But the person who can most effectively explain why your business exists might be the same person doing everything possible to avoid the camera.

Key Point: You don’t have to love attention to become a recognizable leader. Allowing customers to see the person behind a business is one of the simplest ways to build familiarity and trust.

Your logo isn’t a substitute for a human being

Your company probably has a logo, a website, branded social media accounts, and carefully crafted messaging. But does anyone know who’s running the company?

Businesses invest considerable amounts of time and money making their brands look professional. But the people who founded those businesses are often nowhere to be seen.

That’s a huge missed opportunity.

A 2017 study found that presenting human faces alongside brand information can increase brand liking.[1] More recent research published in 2026 found that photographs of real people in social media advertising generated greater credibility and purchase interest than illustrations of people.[2]

Neither study proves that every founder needs to become a spokesperson or talking head, but they do reinforce an important marketing principle: people respond to human connections, even when they’re evaluating brands.

In a Sprout Social consumer survey, 70% of respondents said they felt more connected to a brand when its CEO was active on social media.[3] Connection isn’t a sale, and a social profile isn’t a substitute for good service. But letting people see who’s accountable can make an otherwise faceless company easier to understand.

When customers are buying something that requires trust, that connection is even more important.

Example 1 (RMS Beauty): Cosmetics company RMS Beauty put founder Rose-Marie Swift in front of the camera to share product information and connect with customers on TikTok. Instead of relying solely on traditional product advertising, the brand incorporated candid, founder-led storytelling into its marketing.

According to a case study later published by the social media giant, a campaign featuring Swift alongside paid advertising tools generated 502,000 impressions and a 290% increase in return on ad spend.[4] While those results admittedly reflect the broader campaign, they demonstrate how a founder’s personal presence can contribute to a measurable marketing strategy.

Example 2 (Hello Seven): Hello Seven, founded by Rachel Rodgers, also developed a few distinct social media strategies. Over an eight-and-a-half-month engagement, daily website visits from the company’s Instagram increased from 5 to 91.[5] But most noticeable was the fact that a Reel featuring Rodgers attracted more than 155,000 plays, a strong testament to the power of the founder stepping into the spotlight.[5]

Key Point: Showing the people behind your brand helps customers understand who they’re trusting.

In a world full of AI-generated content, being human is an advantage

Today, almost anyone with a WiFi connection can generate a professional-looking marketing campaign. AI can write blog posts, produce images, create videos, and generate social media captions in seconds.

While it’s useful technology, it also creates a problem for businesses trying to distinguish themselves. When everyone can produce polished content, polish alone becomes less impressive, and consumers have taken notice. In a 2026 Gartner survey, 65% of respondents said brands were producing too much AI-generated marketing content, while 57% said its prevalence had made them less trusting of brand messaging overall.[6]

Just think about everything a founder can contribute that a generic marketing campaign might struggle to replicate. If you’re a construction company owner, you could explain what you’ve learned after 20 years of dealing with difficult job sites. Or a nonprofit director could discuss a problem they’ve encountered while serving their community, or a software founder could explain why their company built a particular feature, including the customer frustrations that inspired it.

The 2026 Edelman–LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report makes a related point: 82% of buyers surveyed said thought leadership was becoming more valuable as AI became a more common research tool, increasing the premium on original research, distinctive perspectives, and human judgment.[7] Useful expertise can help prospective clients consider a business before any sales conversation begins.

Key Point: When anyone can produce professional-looking content with AI, your personal identity becomes a valuable way to distinguish your business.

The spotlight effect: nobody’s thinking about you as much as you think

If you record a video and watch it back, you’ve likely noticed every awkward pause, every strange facial expression, even that one sentence you could have phrased better. You might watch it three times, decide you hate it, then delete the whole thing.

Psychologists have a name for this unpleasant experience: the spotlight effect.

To illustrate:

Spotlight Effect Case Study: In a 2000 study published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, researchers found that people tend to overestimate how much others notice their appearance and behavior. In one experiment, participants wearing potentially embarrassing T-shirts substantially overestimated how many observers would remember what was on those shirts.[10]

The researchers found similar patterns when examining how people evaluated their contributions to group discussions.[10]

Essentially, we’re often much more conscious of our own behavior than everyone else around us is. For founders, this often translates into unnecessary hesitation: What if I sound nervous? What if someone disagrees with me? What if people think I’m full of myself?

Of course, public communication always comes with genuine reputational considerations. People can criticize you, and mistakes can attract attention. But an awkward pause or imperfect delivery isn’t a credibility problem.

Someone watching your video might care more about your explanation of a common customer problem than whether you maintained perfect eye contact.

Key Point: Be mindful of the spotlight effect. Your audience pays far less attention to your camera presence than you do.

Here’s a thought: what if the spotlight isn’t on you?

It might be time to reconsider what public visibility means. For many people, the discomfort isn’t really about the camera.

It’s about what being on camera represents.

They associate personal branding with self-promotion, ego, and people who seem to spend more time talking about their accomplishments than actually doing anything worth talking about.

And that’s fair. We’ve all seen those cringey LinkedIn posts.

But being the person telling a story doesn’t mean you have to be the subject of that story.

You’re representing something far larger than yourself—your employees, your customers, your mission, your community, and the work you’ve spent years building.

Real-World Example: Everlane founder Michael Preysman used personal Instagram posts and the brand’s “Transparency Tuesdays” stories to answer questions about company finances, factory practices, and employee treatment.[8]

The owner, here, is still visible. Customers hear his voice, recognize his face, and learn something about his priorities. He’s there. He’s present. But the subject is the business and the people who make it possible. That’s a much more comfortable starting point for founders who dislike talking about themselves.

And, arguably, it’s more interesting for the audience, too.

Key Point: You don’t have to be the subject of the story to be the person telling it.

The easiest way to promote yourself? Talk about someone else.

If you genuinely hate self-promotion, there’s a simple solution:

Promote someone else.

Recognize an employee who solved a difficult problem, or highlight a customer doing interesting work. Introduce a business partner whose contributions people rarely see. Or talk about an organization your business supports and explain why its mission deserves some attention.

These stories accomplish several things simultaneously. They introduce audiences to the people and organizations around your business. They communicate your values through specific examples. And they create opportunities to strengthen relationships with the people you’re recognizing.

Small-Business Case Study (The Village Confectionary): Before opening in Sleepy Hollow, New York, the founder shared the vision for a nostalgic village candy store and involved local suppliers, tradespeople, and the community.

A later case study reported an Instagram audience exceeding 50,000 before opening and more than 700 customer visits in its first few days. In its first month, sales were more than double the initial forecast.[9]

The results reflected a broader mix of storytelling, community engagement, and local interest—not founder visibility alone.

There’s a lesson here for less photogenic businesses, too: people care about how something gets made, who makes it possible, and why anyone should care. You don’t need a polished personal brand to tell those stories.

Note: One misstep to avoid: charitable work shouldn’t become a convenient excuse to manufacture publicity. Nor should employees, customers, or community partners be treated as props for a marketing campaign.

Ask permission, give credit generously, and make sure the recognition benefits the people involved.

Key Point: Giving someone else the spotlight can, ironically, give visibility to you and your organization.

Camera shy? Start small. Really small.

You don’t need to jump from avoiding publicity to recording daily videos. Start with something more manageable.

1. Put your name behind an idea

Write a short LinkedIn post answering a common customer question. Share a lesson from your industry, or an opinion on a development affecting your customers.

You don’t need a video yet. You don’t even need a photograph. The first step is merely allowing people to associate your name with your expertise.

2. Get comfortable with your own voice

Try recording a 30-second audio explanation or answering a few questions in a recorded interview. You don’t have to publish that first recording.

This exercise just helps you become more comfortable speaking without rehearsing every sentence until it sounds like a stiff corporate press release.

3. Show up without making a production of it

Record a quick video from your office or job site. Explain something useful, or introduce someone.

Again, you don’t need expensive equipment, dramatic transitions, or a produced introduction worthy of an Emmy. A phone, decent lighting, and something worthwhile to say are plenty.

4. Make visibility part of the routine

Choose a realistic cadence. It might be one founder-authored post every other week or one short video per month.

Consistency gives people repeated opportunities to recognize you while giving you opportunities to improve.

Key Point: Public visibility is a skill. You can develop that skill gradually without forcing yourself into a role that feels unnatural.

You don’t need to become an influencer. You need to become recognizable.

You can have a useful public presence without daily videos, a huge following, or a personal confession every Tuesday. You don’t need to share your deepest, darkest secrets. You just need the people most relevant to your business to recognize you, understand what you know, and feel comfortable starting a conversation.

A couple of months ago, we published an article called You Don’t Need to Go Viral: What Good Marketing Actually Looks Like. That same principle applies to founder visibility: attention from the right people is more useful than attention from everyone.

Choose channels where your customers already spend time. That might mean a trade conference, a community newsletter, local media, professional associations, a podcast, or LinkedIn. Different businesses have different audiences. A public platform is a way to reach them, not the objective itself.

And establish boundaries: you don’t need to share your children, your vacations, or every detail of your day.

Key Point: Your customers don’t need access to your personal life. They need opportunities to understand who they’re doing business with.

A practical framework for founders who would rather stay behind the scenes

If you’re still uncomfortable with public visibility, remember one word: SHOW.

STEP 1: START

  • Start with something you know.
  • Answer a question your customers ask regularly.
  • Share something you’ve learned.
  • Explain a common misconception.
  • Expertise gives you something concrete to discuss.

STEP 2: HIGHLIGHT

  • Highlight your mission and other people.
  • Introduce your employees, recognize partners, or explain why your company supports causes.
  • Give your audience a window into the organization without making yourself the center of attention.

STEP 3: OWN

  • Own your perspective.
  • Don’t just repeat the same generic industry advice.
  • Explain what you believe, why you believe it, and what experience has taught you.
  • Your perspective won’t resonate with everyone. But it doesn’t need to.

STEP 4: WORK UP

  • Work your way up.
  • Start with a written post, then move to audio. Then record a short video.
  • Participate in an interview. Consider speaking at an event.
  • There’s no requirement to master every format.

The idea is to become progressively more comfortable with the kinds of visibility that serve your business.

And remember: none of this means every founder must become the company’s sole spokesperson. Your employees and leadership team can share the responsibility.

Conclusion: Stop letting your logo do all the talking

Remember that 30-second introductory video you couldn’t bring yourself to record?

You’ve been overthinking it.

You don’t need a perfect script, professional lighting, or the confidence of someone who thrives on being the center of attention. You just need something useful to share, and a willingness to let people hear it from you.

You built the business. You understand its purpose, its people, and the problems it solves.

Don’t make your customers rely entirely on a logo and carefully written About page to learn all those things.

Here’s our challenge: publish one thing this week that lets people hear directly from the person behind your business. You can answer a question from a customer, recognize an employee, tell a story about why your work matters to the people you serve.

It doesn’t have to be about you.

But it should come from you.
