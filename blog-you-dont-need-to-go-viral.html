You don't need to go viral: what good marketing actually looks like.

Going viral looks like the best possible outcome. For almost every business, it isn't the goal — and chasing it can actively work against you. Here's what the research on audience targeting says to focus on instead.

How to read this

Built for skimming, not required reading front to back.

This piece is intentionally skim-friendly. If you only read the headings, the bold text and the examples, you'll walk away with the main ideas — we designed it that way on purpose, because concepts matter more than the individual words. Read all of it, half of it, or just the bold parts. As long as you leave with the main idea, it did its job.

Why most businesses do not need to "go viral"

"Going viral" has become one of the easiest ways to describe successful marketing. A video gets a million views. A brand gains thousands of followers overnight. A campaign gets reposted everywhere. From the outside, it looks like the perfect outcome.

But for most businesses, it is not the goal. A local restaurant does not need the whole internet talking about it. A law firm does not need millions of followers. A plumber does not need to become a household name. They need the right customers to notice them, trust them, and take action.

Key point

Good marketing is not about the biggest audience. It's about the right one.

Nielsen's research on audience targeting backs this up: across 82 digital campaigns, the company found a strong relationship between reaching the intended audience and better sales outcomes. Partners that most effectively reached the target audience generated an average $2.60 in ROI for every $1 spent, compared with just $0.25 among partners that underdelivered to the target audience — more than a 10x difference.[1]

In great marketing, relevance beats fame

Some of the best-marketed businesses are companies most people have never heard of. They may dominate a neighborhood, an industry, or a specific customer segment while remaining almost invisible outside of it. That is not a weakness — in many cases, it is exactly what successful marketing looks like.

Example (HVAC): Consider Cardinal Heating & Air, a Kirkland-based HVAC company serving the greater Seattle area. Most people outside the Pacific Northwest have probably never heard of it. But within the market that matters, Cardinal built a powerful reputation.

Founded in 1991, Cardinal became what investment bank Alexander Hutton described as a "prominent brand in Seattle HVAC," earning its reputation through high-quality work, customer service, and the ability to solve complex HVAC problems.[2] The company did not need national name recognition to build a valuable business. It needed homeowners in its market to know the Cardinal name, trust it, and choose it when they needed heating and cooling services.

That local reputation ultimately attracted private equity. Cardinal was acquired by Chicago-based SE Capital Partners, which described it as a respected Northwest brand and a strong addition to its portfolio of HVAC service companies, and as an entry into what it considered the desirable Pacific Northwest market.[2]

Cardinal did not need millions of followers or nationwide recognition to become an attractive acquisition target. It built something arguably more valuable: a trusted brand among the customers it actually served. That is the difference between fame and relevance. A business can be virtually unknown to most of the country while becoming highly valuable within the market that actually matters.

The right audience beats the biggest audience (backed by evidence)

Reach is attractive because it is easy to show. "Your campaign reached 500,000 people" sounds impressive. The better question is: who were those 500,000 people?

If a small, single-location restaurant reaches one million people across the country, the number may look great. But most of those people cannot realistically become customers. Now compare that with reaching 1,000 people who live nearby, eat out regularly, and are interested in that type of food. The second audience is smaller, but it is far more useful.

Research from Boston Consulting Group helps put a number behind that difference. BCG found that personalized offers — designed around the interests and behaviors of specific customers — can generate approximately 3x the ROI of mass promotions.[3] The lesson is not that every campaign needs to be personalized down to the individual. It is that relevance has economic value: the closer the audience and message are to the people most likely to buy, the more valuable each impression can become.

Case study

Expensya: broad reach vs. narrow relevance

Expense-management software company Expensya runs two kinds of campaigns: broad ones designed to reach and nurture a larger pool of potential customers, and much narrower ones aimed at high-intent decision-makers such as CEOs, CFOs, and accountants.[4] The broad campaigns generate about four times the lead volume. But the narrower campaigns are twice as efficient at generating leads, with Lead Gen Form completion rates roughly two times higher than those from broader, colder audiences.[4]

That distinction matters. If the objective were simply to generate the largest possible number of impressions or names in a database, the broader campaign would appear to win. But if the objective is to efficiently reach people who are closer to becoming customers, the smaller and more relevant audience performs better. This is why audience size can be a misleading measure of marketing success. A campaign that reaches fewer people can still create more value if those people are substantially more likely to become customers. Put differently: fewer impressions do not necessarily mean less opportunity. Sometimes they mean less wasted attention.

Even influencers need the right audience

Influencers might seem like the exception to this rule. After all, attention is part of their business — more followers can mean more views, more sponsorship opportunities, and greater earning potential. But even here, the quality and relevance of the audience still matter.

An influencer with two million followers is not necessarily more valuable to a brand than one with 100,000. If those 100,000 people are deeply interested in a particular subject, trust the creator, and regularly act on their recommendations, that smaller audience can be extraordinarily valuable.

Research published in the Journal of Marketing illustrates this tradeoff. Researchers analyzed 802 Instagram marketing campaigns involving more than 1,700 influencers, supplemented by eye-tracking and laboratory experiments. They found that the relationship between follower count and engagement was not simply "more is better." Instead, engagement followed an inverted U-shape: adding followers initially increased engagement, but at sufficiently high follower counts, engagement began to decline — larger audiences created weaker perceived relationships between influencers and their followers, making each individual follower less likely to engage.[5]

A broader meta-analysis published in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science reviewed 251 papers and 1,531 effect sizes on influencer marketing. It found that follower characteristics — particularly how strongly followers identify with the influencer and their community — were among the strongest drivers of consumer attitudes and engagement, alongside influencer-brand fit, credibility, content quality and communication with followers.[6] Research on product-influencer fit reaches a similar conclusion: in an experiment involving 432 Instagram users, a large following alone was not enough. When the promoted product fit the influencer's established identity, followers perceived the influencer as more credible and identified more strongly with them, which contributed to stronger advertising outcomes and purchase intentions.[7]

For influencers, then, virality is not useless — it is most valuable when it attracts more of the right people. A creator who repeatedly goes viral with content unrelated to their core audience may accumulate followers without building a particularly valuable community. A creator who becomes deeply trusted within a specific niche can build an audience that brands want access to, even if it never becomes enormous. The goal is not simply to be known by more people. It is to matter more to the right people.

Virality can become a problem when a business is not ready

There is another side of virality that gets discussed much less often: what happens if it actually works? Marketing can create demand much faster than a business can create employees, inventory, appointments, production capacity, or customer-service infrastructure. A post can reach millions of people overnight. The rest of the business cannot always scale at the same speed.

Real-world example

Okonomi Asian Grill and the "Keith Lee effect"

After TikTok food critic Keith Lee reviewed Okonomi Asian Grill, a small restaurant in Fairfax, Virginia, the fallout was immediate. Before the review, the restaurant typically handled no more than about 80 orders a day. After the video posted, roughly 400 orders came through in a single day — about five times normal volume.[8] Supplies ran low, certain ingredients sold out shortly after opening, and only a small number of employees had been scheduled to work. The owner eventually had to ask regular customers to temporarily stay away unless they ordered ahead and were prepared for longer waits.[8]

This does not mean going viral was bad for the restaurant — quite the opposite: the attention introduced the business to hundreds of new customers and created enormous demand. But it illustrates an important distinction: generating demand and servicing demand are two different things. A business only benefits from new attention if it has the capacity to convert that attention into a good customer experience.

Harvard Business School professor Gary Pisano makes a similar argument in his research on business growth. After studying nearly 11,000 publicly traded U.S. companies and conducting case studies across more than 20 companies, Pisano argues that sustainable growth requires balancing market demand with the organizational capabilities needed to serve that demand.[9] When demand grows faster than staffing, systems, training, inventory, or operational capacity, the result can be quality problems, overwhelmed employees, poor customer service, and ultimately damage to the reputation that created the demand in the first place.[9]

Virality simply compresses that problem into a much shorter period of time. A restaurant might suddenly have a line around the block. An e-commerce company might receive months' worth of orders in a weekend. A local service business might receive hundreds of calls when it only has enough technicians to complete a few dozen jobs. In each case, the marketing worked — the business was simply not ready for how well it worked.

None of this means businesses should fear attention or avoid opportunities to go viral. It means businesses should build the foundation necessary to benefit from that attention when it comes. Before chasing the next million views, ask a less exciting but potentially more valuable question: if a million people saw us tomorrow, would we actually be ready for what happens next? Good marketing creates demand. Great businesses are prepared to capture and service it.

So what should businesses and marketers do instead of chasing virality?

If the goal is not simply to reach as many people as possible, what should businesses and marketers actually optimize for? The answer is not to avoid reach. It is to become more deliberate about who you are trying to reach, what you want them to do, and whether the business is prepared when they respond. We think every campaign should run through the same four steps:

  • Define. The desired business outcome, the metrics that will determine success, and the "right audience" the campaign is intended to reach.
  • Target. Identify the channels where that audience already spends time, build the message and creative around what matters to them, and focus resources on reaching the most relevant people — not simply the largest number.
  • Service. Make sure the business is prepared to capture and serve the demand the campaign creates — a clear path from attention to action, and a website, sales process, staffing and inventory that can support the response.
  • Measure. Track performance beyond views, impressions, likes and followers. Measure what happens further down the funnel: qualified leads, conversions, acquisition cost, revenue, retention and repeat business — then use it to refine the audience, message, channels and investment over time.

A helpful shorthand for that is "D-T-S-M": Define, Target, Service, Measure. Not a catchy acronym — our job is to be helpful, not catchy.

Food for thought: do these principles still apply in the age of AI search?

There is one obvious question left: does any of this change in the age of AI? Increasingly, customers are not simply scrolling through ten blue links and deciding which website looks most relevant. Google AI Overviews, AI Mode, and other generative search tools can now interpret a question, gather information from multiple sources, and provide an answer before the user ever visits a website.

That change is already significant. Pew Research Center found that 60% of U.S. adults say they read AI summaries at the top of search results.[10] An earlier Pew analysis found that when a Google search produced an AI summary, users clicked a traditional search result only 8% of the time, compared with 15% when no AI summary appeared — and links cited directly inside the AI summary were clicked in only about 1% of visits.[11] These trends are accelerating what marketers sometimes call the "zero-click" era: a growing share of searches end without the user ever visiting a website at all.

So yes, AI changes how businesses get discovered. But we'd argue it makes the principles above more important, not less — AI adds another layer of relevance, it doesn't remove relevance altogether.

Imagine someone searching "Who is the best HVAC company for replacing a heat pump in Kirkland?" Traditionally, Google would return a list of websites, ads, maps and reviews for the customer to evaluate. Today, an AI-powered search experience may do part of that evaluation first: interpret the question, retrieve information from multiple sources, compare those sources, and synthesize an answer. That means businesses increasingly operate across two layers of relevance: first, you need to be relevant to the customer; second, your content and online presence need to be relevant to the AI systems helping that customer find an answer.

That does not mean marketers should abandon everything they know about good marketing and start writing for robots — Google's own guidance says essentially the opposite. Google states that its generative AI search features are built on its existing Search ranking and quality systems, using techniques like retrieval-augmented generation and "query fan-out," where the system runs multiple related searches to answer a user's broader question.[12] Its recommendation for succeeding in AI search sounds remarkably similar to what marketers should already be doing: create unique, useful, reliable, people-first content; keep the site technically accessible and business information current; and provide original expertise instead of reproducing what's already out there. Google specifically warns businesses against chasing supposed AI-search hacks at the expense of creating genuinely useful content.[12]

In other words: don't optimize for AI at the expense of people. Make it easier for AI to understand why you're valuable to the people you're trying to reach. If anything, AI search strengthens the case for focusing on the right audience, because the questions people ask it are far more specific. Traditional search often centered on a short keyword — "Seattle HVAC." Generative search lets customers ask something like: "I have a 2,500-square-foot house in Kirkland with an old gas furnace. What type of heat pump should I consider, how much should installation cost, and which local companies specialize in these conversions?" That query reveals considerably more about the customer's needs and intent — and it's an opportunity. Instead of producing generic content aimed at the broadest possible keyword, a business can create genuinely useful content around the specific questions its ideal customers actually face. A contractor might publish detailed heat-pump installation case studies. A law firm might explain a highly specific financing structure its startup clients regularly encounter. A software company might publish original data on how customers in a particular industry use its product. The audience for each piece may be small — but once again, the right audience matters far more than the biggest one.

AI visibility is becoming part of marketing, but it should not become another vanity metric. Being cited by an AI system is useful only if it ultimately contributes to a meaningful business outcome — which brings us right back to the same framework: Define what the business is actually trying to accomplish and understand the questions its ideal customers are asking. Target those customers with useful information in the channels and formats through which they discover businesses, including AI-powered search. Service the customer when that discovery turns into an inquiry, visit, purchase or appointment. Measure whether that visibility ultimately produces meaningful results.

The technology between a business and its customer will keep changing; the underlying objective does not. Whether someone discovers you through Google, TikTok, an influencer, ChatGPT, an AI Overview, or something that doesn't exist yet, good marketing still comes down to the same thing: be useful to the right people when they need you. In the age of AI, the goal is not to appear in every answer. It is to be a good answer when the right customer asks the right question.

Conclusion: stop chasing vanity metrics

Once again, virality is not bad. If a campaign reaches millions of the right people, creates meaningful demand, and the business is prepared to serve that demand, that can be incredibly valuable. The mistake is treating virality as the default definition of good marketing.

Views, impressions, followers and reach can all matter — but only if they connect to something the business actually cares about: more qualified leads, more bookings, more purchases, better retention, more revenue. That's why we think good marketing starts with a different question. Not "how do we get as many people as possible to see this?" but "who actually needs to see this, what do we want them to do, and what happens when they do it?"

That shift moves the focus from fame to relevance. From audience size to audience quality. From impressions to outcomes. From chasing a temporary spike in attention to building a repeatable system for growth — which is also the logic behind the framework above: Define what success actually looks like. Target the people most likely to matter. Make sure the business can serve the demand you create. Then measure whether the campaign actually moved the business forward.

Because a business does not need everyone to know its name. It needs enough of the right people to know it, trust it, choose it, and come back. You do not need to go viral — you need marketing that works.
